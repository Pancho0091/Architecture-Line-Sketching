Line technique

RuleA straight line is not drawn by one joint. Any single joint rotating alone draws an arc; a straight line comes from the shoulder and elbow moving together, planned before the pen lands and executed in one committed stroke. Train that motor pattern first, in isolation, until it is boringly repeatable. Everything later in the program is built out of it.

This is step one. Do the Foundation stages (2 weeks, 20 min a day) before Phase 1 of the curriculum, then keep the 10-minute warm-up before every session for the rest of the program. A weekly test tells you, with numbers, whether it is working.

1 Setup: grip, posture, page

Grip
Keep your normal writing grip; in studies of children, four mature grips gave the same speed, legibility and force (Schwellnus et al.). Change what moves, not how you hold. Relaxed: “do not squeeze the pen” (Ching). Hold about 1–2 cm from the tip for detail and hatching, 3–5 cm for long lines [rule of thumb].
Pen angle
Fineliners (Micron, Copic Multiliner): hold like a normal pen, about 60–90° [rule of thumb]; steel-tube technical pens at 90° (Cult Pens). A low angle drags the side of the nib: wider, rougher line and uneven wear.
Pressure
Writing pressure or less. On a Micron PN, heavy pressure widens a 0.4 mm line to about 0.6 mm (Larry Post), so pressure breaks consistency and wears the nib. Weight comes from nib size, never from pressing.
Posture
Sit upright, feet flat, elbow off the table (a planted elbow becomes a pivot; Drawabox discourages it). Resting the side of the hand lightly on the page is fine. Paper slightly toward your drawing side so the arm swings without hitting your body.
Left-handed?
Mirror everything: your best direction is usually a pull toward you or a right-to-left sweep; rotate the page clockwise rather than counter-clockwise; work left-to-right across the page only if you can keep your hand off wet ink (pigment fineliners dry in seconds, but rest on a scrap sheet). A hooked wrist is fine as long as the wrist doesn't rotate through long strokes.
Paper
Drills and warm-ups go on printer paper: cheap enough that you won't be precious about a page. Weekly tests always use the same paper and the same pen, so results compare week to week. Sketchbook is for applied drawings.
Page
Rotate the page, not your body: find your single best stroke direction (usually a pull toward you, or a left-to-right sweep for right-handers) and turn the page so every line uses it. A flat page seen at an angle foreshortens your drawing; tilt the book 15–30° or check by holding it upright now and then.
Light and state
Light from the side opposite your drawing hand. Tremor (8–12 Hz) rises with caffeine, fatigue, anxiety and right after exercise (FPnotebook): do measured tests before the second espresso.

2 Joints: why lines curve

Rule
A joint is a compass: the pen tip moves on a circle whose radius is the joint-to-tip distance. A chord of length L on radius r bows by r − √(r² − (L/2)²) ≈ L²/8r. So the bow grows with the square of the stroke length and falls with the radius.
Analogy
Your arm is a two-link robot arm. One rotating joint can only sweep an arc; a straight tool path needs two joints solving for it together (inverse kinematics). The shoulder swings, the elbow opens, and their two arcs cancel. That coordination is the skill.
Exception
Short marks. Below about 3.5 cm even the wrist bows less than 1 mm, and the wrist is more precise; use it for hatching and texture, which Ching builds from relatively short strokes.

3 The stroke: plan, ghost, execute

  1. Plan. Place both endpoints as dots (or crosses). The line now has a target at each end.
  2. Rotate. Turn the page so the line runs in your best direction. Pull, don't push (Ching).
  3. Ghost 3×. Run the exact motion with the tip a few mm above the paper, from the shoulder, until it passes over both dots. You are rehearsing the joint pattern.
  4. Execute. Land the tip on the start dot already moving and draw at ghosting speed, about 0.5–1 s for 15 cm. Eyes on the end dot, not the nib. Constant speed and pressure; breathe out.
  5. Lift off in motion, through or just past the end dot. Small overshoot at corners (1–3 mm, “architect's crossed corners”) is acceptable; hooks and blobs are not.
  6. Leave it. No re-tracing, no patching. Judge it, then draw the next one.
Speed variable
Too slow: the eye steers, and every correction shows as wobble. Too fast: scatter grows roughly with velocity and you miss the dot (Schmidt's law). The target is the fastest speed that still lands. Falsifiable: draw five lines at ~3 s and five at ~0.7 s. Prediction: the slow set ripples more; the fast set misses endpoints more.
Weight variable
Weight comes from nib size at constant pressure. Hierarchy (after Ching and the Memphis standards, translated to Micron sizes; Memphis uses technical-pen widths, e.g. 0.25 for light lines): 05 for profiles and the nearest edges, 03 for planar corners, 01 for interior and shallow changes, 005 for texture and distance. Ching's “decisive” ends (a slight press at start and finish) are an advanced option, not a blob.
Exception
A deliberately broken or trembling line is a texture (old timber, weathered stone). The rules govern lines meant to be straight.

8 Measure yourself

Weekly test
Every Monday, rested, before the warm-up, on printer paper with the same pen every week: 20 lines, 15 cm, dot to dot (pencil dots placed with a ruler). Week 0 is your baseline. After week 2, mix the directions.
Protocol A · ruler
Endpoint miss: ruler from dot to line end. Max deviation: lay a clear ruler along the line's own two ends and read the biggest gap (± 0.5 mm). Note the bow's side (+ toward you / − away). Pass = deviation ≤ 2 mm and endpoint ≤ 2 mm (week 12 target: ≤ 1.5 mm).
Bias vs spread
Same-side bows on most lines = bias: a pivot or posture problem; fix with elbow lift, page rotation. Random sides = spread: a planning or speed problem; fix with more ghosting or 10–20% slower. Track them separately: it's accuracy vs precision.
Feedback rule
On practice days don't measure everything: guess your worst line first, then check only lines that look over ~2 mm. Constant feedback makes you dependent on it.
One ladder
The page uses three bars, on purpose: Foundation gate (end of week 2): ≥ 60% of 15 cm lines within 2 mm. Phase 1 checkpoint (curriculum week 3): the rubric's 1 mm test on 5–10 cm drill lines; shorter lines bow far less (bow grows with length²), so 1 mm there is about as hard as 2 mm on 15 cm. Week 12: the progression table's ≤ 1.5 mm on 15 cm. Until a bar is due, log the trend instead of judging.
Stuck rule
If three weekly tests show no change, change one variable (speed, joint, page rotation, pen) rather than adding volume.
Protocol B · phone photo + Python (± 0.2 mm; for the analyst)

Photograph the page from directly overhead (a level app helps), about 30 cm away, with a ruler on the page. PX_PER_MM = pixels between the 0 and 100 mm marks ÷ 100. Needs pip install numpy pillow scipy. Validate first: a ruler-drawn line should read ≤ 0.3 mm; that is your noise floor. Tested on synthetic pages (a 1 mm bow reads 0.9–1.0 mm). Limits: crop the ruler out before running (it would count as a line); lines that cross merge into one blob, so keep test lines apart; it measures straightness, not endpoint miss (use the ruler for that). Default pass threshold is 2 mm, matching Protocol A.

Line Technique

Shoulder and elbow together, wrist quiet, planned before the pen lands.

F
Key idea

Any one joint draws an arc. A straight line is two joints cancelling each other's arcs, rehearsed (ghosted) and then executed as one fast, committed stroke. Practice the motion, not the line.

How it works
  1. Elbow off the table; page rotated to your best direction.
  2. Dot both ends.
  3. Ghost 3× from the shoulder.
  4. Land moving, eyes on the end dot, 0.5–1 s.
  5. Lift off past the dot. Never re-trace.
Common mistakes
  • Wrist driving long lines: bow
  • Slow, steered stroke: ripple
  • Re-tracing a miss: hair
  • Braking near the end: wobbly tail
  • Pressing for weight: wide line, crushed nib
Practice
  • Week 1: superimposed, ghosted, long lines
  • Week 2: planes, parallels, grid connect
  • Then a 10-min warm-up, forever
Test
  • 20 lines × 15 cm, Mondays
  • Pass: ≤ 2 mm deviation and ≤ 2 mm endpoint miss
  • Target: ≥ 80% by week 12

Phase 1 · Lesson sheet

Line Control

One pass, a locked joint, and the fastest stroke that still lands.

01
Key idea

A line's quality is decided before the pen touches the paper: by the rehearsal (ghosting) and by which joint drives the stroke. Draw lines; don't steer them.

How it works
  1. Place both endpoints as dots.
  2. Rotate the page so the stroke runs in your most comfortable direction.
  3. Ghost 3× above the paper: elbow for medium lines, shoulder for long ones.
  4. Lower the pen and commit in one pass, at the fastest speed that still lands.
  5. Lift off at the end. Never go back over the line.
Line weight

Weight comes from the nib, not pressure: 05 for silhouettes and the ground line, 03 for secondary edges, 01 for detail and hatching, 005 for distance and texture. A flick (lift off while moving) gives a taper. Exception: if you're unsure of a contour, ink it in 01 first and restate it in 05 at the end.

Exception: with graphite, pressure is a fair way to vary weight. With a fineliner it only crushes the nib, so switch pens instead.

Common mistakes
  • Drawing long lines from the wrist: they curve into arcs
  • Gripping hard or pressing: shaky lines, crushed nib
  • Hesitating mid-stroke: a broken line
  • Re-tracing a missed line: a hairy line that marks the miss
  • Rushing past the endpoint: overshoot
Practice

Parallel lines across a full page of printer paper, no ruler, about 8 mm apart. Then a page at 45° and a page of verticals.

Score: count lines that touch or cross a neighbour. Pass: at most 3 touching or crossing pairs per 25 lines by day 5.

Understanding Texture

Texture is value plus pattern, so it obeys every Phase 2 rule.

02
Key idea

Every material reaches the eye through two signals: how dark it is (ink coverage) and what its marks look like (pattern). Get the value right first; the pattern only tells the eye which material it is. Analog: value is the measure, pattern is the categorical label.

Texture types
Smooth
Plaster, concrete, painted wood. Long, sparse parallel lines; lots of white.
Rough
Stone, stucco, bark. Stipple and broken outlines; small, sharp shadows.
Patterned
Brick, tile, cladding. Draw the joints, not every unit, and let the pattern fade away from the focal point.
Reflective
Glass, polished metal. The highest contrast in the drawing: dark panes with crisp white streaks, sharp mullions.
Observation notes
  • Light: texture shows in raking light (light skimming across the surface) and flattens in frontal light, however bright.
  • Direction: follow the grain or courses: horizontal for brick and clapboard, vertical for board-and-batten.
  • Scale: units shrink with distance; far away, draw every second or third joint.
  • Contrast: put the darkest darks next to the brightest reflections.
Exception

Many guides teach texture with pencil pressure (light, medium, heavy). That's right for graphite. In ink the same job is done by line spacing and layers, which is what the matrix above shows.

03

Phase 3 · Lesson sheet

Looking Up & Down

The third vanishing point: what happens when you tilt your view.

04
Key idea

Tilt your view and vertical edges start receding too, so they converge on a third, vertical vanishing point (VVP). Looking up: horizon low, VVP above. Looking down: horizon high, VVP below.

How it works
  1. Place the horizon: low on the page to look up, high to look down.
  2. Put the left and right vanishing points on it, far apart.
  3. Put the VVP on a vertical line, well above or below the page.
  4. Draw the nearest vertical edge first. Aim every vertical at the VVP and every horizontal at its side vanishing point.
  5. Check: extend three verticals. They should meet at one point.
2-point vs. 3-point

In 2-point perspective, verticals stay parallel because your view is level. 3-point is the exception the Phase 3 rule warns about: correct when you tilt your view, wrong when you don't.

When to switch (rule of thumb): once your view tilts more than about 15°, or the subject fills more than about 60° of your vertical view. Exception: architectural drawings often keep verticals parallel for small tilts, like a shift lens, because converging verticals read as leaning.

Analog: the VVP is the same projection applied to the vertical direction vector.

Phone photos of tall buildings are 3-point by accident. That's why the week 9 photos must be shot level.

Common mistakes
  • Putting the VVP too close: towers turn into pyramids
  • Mixing parallel and converging verticals in one drawing
  • Forgetting that horizontals still go to the side vanishing points
Practice

Two drawings as boxes first: one tower looking up from the sidewalk, one block looking down from a rooftop.

Test: extend 3 verticals in each; they should meet within 2% of the page width of one VVP.

Quick review

RememberA level view means parallel verticals; a tilted view means a third vanishing point.

Building Ornament

Detail on top of structure: envelope, axis, shadow.

05
Key idea

Ornament is the last layer of the pipeline, never the first. Box its envelope, set the symmetry axis, then let shadow shapes, not outlines, carry the carving.

How it works
  1. Envelope: box the whole ornament in pencil.
  2. Axis: draw the center line; most ornament is symmetric.
  3. Big shapes: circles for volutes, arcs for moldings.
  4. Contours in 01, only where an edge turns.
  5. Shadow: hatch the undersides. That's what makes it read as carved.
Vocabulary
Cornice
The projecting crown at the top of a wall.
Dentils
A row of small blocks under a cornice.
Frieze
The band below the cornice, often carved.
Capital
The head of a column or pilaster.
Pilaster
A flat column attached to a wall.
Keystone
The central wedge at the top of an arch.
Pediment hood
A triangular or curved cap over a window or door.
Tips
  • At a distance, reduce ornament to rhythm: dentils become a dashed line, a frieze a texture band.
  • Draw one half, then check the other against the axis.
  • Put full ornament only at the focal point (Selection).
Common mistakes
  • Outlining every leaf
  • Starting with detail before the envelope
  • Ornament everywhere at equal detail
Quick review

Practice: find a cornice, capital or keystone and draw it in the four passes. LA's historic Broadway theater district is full of them; a level phone photo works too. Test: flip your drawing horizontally; the halves should match within 2 mm of the axis, and someone else should tell projecting from recessed parts in at least 4 of 5 elements. Analog: envelope = bounding box, axis = schema, contours = the data.

RememberShadow carves; outline flattens.

Concept Sketches

Ideas first, details later: rectangles and triangles only.

06
Key idea

A concept sketch tests one massing idea in under two minutes. It's a prototype, not a deliverable: speed and quantity beat accuracy, which is why it's the one place this program lets lines stay loose.

How it works
  1. Set a timer: 2 minutes per concept.
  2. Start with the elevation: a rectangle plus a triangle or two.
  3. Push it into 2-point perspective as boxes.
  4. Hatch one side so the volume reads.
  5. Move to the next concept; don't fix the last one.
Technical analog

This is rapid prototyping: generate many cheap variants, then develop one, the way you'd sketch several query plans before optimizing the winner.

Common mistakes
  • Detailing too soon
  • Erasing instead of starting a new concept
  • Five versions of the same idea
Practice

Concept sprint: 5 massings in 10 minutes, using only rectangles and triangles. Circle the best one; it becomes your next thumbnail.

Test: blind-rank each sprint the next day. Over 4 sprints the "fifth idea" claim holds if the winner's average position is 3rd or later.

Quick review

RememberQuantity builds quality; the fifth idea usually beats the first.

Phase 4 · Lesson sheet · week 11

People in Perspective

The horizon rule: eyes on the horizon, size from distance.

07
Key idea

When you stand on the same level ground as the people you draw, every standing adult's eyes sit on the horizon line, near or far. Distance changes their size, never where the horizon crosses them.

How it works
  1. Draw the horizon (your eye level) and the vanishing point.
  2. Place the nearest figure: feet on the ground, eyes on the horizon.
  3. For each new figure, pick the feet point; its eyes still go on the horizon.
  4. Block figures as simple shapes: head, torso, legs.
  5. Overlap a few and vary the spacing; avoid neat rows.
Exceptions

The rule assumes you and the figures share one level ground plane. Change your own height and the horizon still crosses every standing adult at the same body point, just not the eyes: about two-thirds of the way up (belly height) when you sit, above their heads from a second floor. On a slope or stairs the rule breaks: uphill figures' eyes sit above the horizon and downhill figures' below, because they stand on a different plane. Children are shorter, so the horizon passes over their heads.

Common mistakes
  • On flat ground while standing, figures' heads at different heights
  • Over-detailed figures: they're scale markers, not portraits
  • Evenly spaced rows of people
Practice

Add 3–5 people to a street scene with the horizon rule.

Test: lay a ruler along the horizon. It should cross every standing adult at the eyes, within 1 mm. Analog: horizon height is your eye height, a constant joined to every figure.

Quick review

RememberPeople are a scale bar. Get them right and the building's size reads instantly.

Technique Library

Ways to put tone and form on paper, and when each one wins.

09
Key idea

Every technique is the same function with different arguments: mark shape, spacing, direction and layers in; tone and texture out. Pick a technique by what you need the output to say (flat value, form, material, speed), not by what looks impressive.

Practice

Shade the same simple box three times with three different techniques, aiming for the same three tones (light top, mid left, dark right).

Test: photograph all three in grayscale. If the matching faces read as the same value, you control tone independently of technique. If not, the technique is driving you.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing three or more techniques in one small area
  • Choosing stipple for large areas: beautiful and very slow
  • Hatch direction that ignores the surface it describes

RememberTechnique is a tool for value, never a substitute for it.
