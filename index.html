TrapThe size code isn't the width. Micron "005" draws 0.20 mm, not 0.05 mm. Other brands map codes differently (Pilot's small sizes run finer than Micron's), so compare pens by millimeters.

RuleNever press harder for a darker line. Pressure crushes fineliner nibs, and the finest tips are the most fragile. Darker = bigger nib or more lines.

Already have a pencil set?Use the hardest grade you can still see without pressing; that's usually 2H, or HB if 2H is too faint. Harder than about 4H dents the paper and ink catches in the grooves; 2B and softer smear and leave a waxy layer ink can skip on, so keep them for thumbnails and value studies you won't ink. Test: on a scrap of your sketchbook, draw a line with each grade, ink across them with your pen, wait 10 minutes and erase. Keep the hardest grade that was clearly visible, left no dent and erased fully.

Only have one pen?Start anyway: all of Phase 1 works with a single nib. With a 0.35 mm pen (the width of a Micron 03), fake week 3's weights: two strokes side by side for silhouettes, one stroke for detail, a broken line or pencil for distance. In Phase 2 each layer covers more paper, so space hatching wider (about 0.7 mm for near-black with 4 crossed layers). Buy next in this order: 01 (your hatching pen), then 005, then 05.

The governing model

RuleA pen-and-ink drawing is the output of four separable subsystems; most bad drawings fail in exactly one. They have dependencies (Line feeds Value and Structure; both feed Selection), so train them in dependency order, each in isolation (unit tests), then integrate (integration tests).

drawing = render(
    structure,   # where things are: perspective, proportion, placement
    line,        # how each mark behaves: straightness, weight, confidence
    value,       # light vs dark, built ONLY from ink coverage (no grey ink)
    selection    # what you leave out: edges, detail, emphasis
)

Key constraintInk is append-only. You can't erase, so the workflow is a two-pass pipeline: a light pencil underdrawing (staging, where errors are cheap) then ink (the irreversible commit). Skipping pass one is the single biggest source of ruined pages.

ExceptionExperienced urban sketchers often ink with no pencil. That works only after structure is internalized, which is the end state of this program, not the start.

Skill tree

RulePhases are ordered by dependency, not difficulty: a phase depends only on the phases with arrows into it. Value and Perspective each need only Line; Composition needs both.

Value and perspective both require clean lines, which is why line drills come first even though they feel the least like "drawing."

12-week curriculum

Four phases of three weeks. Each week = 5 weekday sessions of 45 min + a Sunday master copy (6 sessions, about 4.5 hours). Every phase is specified the same way: rule, variables, exceptions, drills, a falsifiable prediction, and a checkpoint. Advance only when the checkpoint passes the rubric for the subsystems trained so far; if it fails, follow the failed-checkpoint rule in Verification.

Sequencing: weeks 1–6 Apply pieces (and the week 6 house) are flat elevations; perspective arrives in week 7. From week 3, mark one focal point on every Apply piece; it isn't scored until Phase 4. Expect to pass the checkpoints, not to be fluent: 12 weeks is about 54 hours, so plan a second cycle.

01 Line control weeks 1–3

Rule
Straightness = speed × a locked joint, up to a ceiling. Hesitation shows up as wobble; re-tracing shows up as hair; too much speed shows up as overshoot. Use the fastest stroke that still lands on the endpoint.
Variables
Stroke speed, which joint moves (wrist for short, elbow for medium, shoulder for long), number of ghost reps, nib (use 01 for drills).
Exception
A deliberately slow, broken line is a valid texture (old wood, weathered stone). The rule governs lines meant to be straight.

Lab 01 Stroke lab

Watch 3 ghost passes (the pen hovering above the paper), then the committed stroke. Slow strokes wobble; very fast ones overshoot the endpoint. The readout measures both against the 1 mm rubric line (the line is about 12 cm long at A5 scale).

Phase 1 · Lesson sheet

Line Control

One pass, a locked joint, and the fastest stroke that still lands.

01
Key idea

A line's quality is decided before the pen touches the paper: by the rehearsal (ghosting) and by which joint drives the stroke. Draw lines; don't steer them.

How it works
  1. Place both endpoints as dots.
  2. Rotate the page so the stroke runs in your most comfortable direction.
  3. Ghost 3× above the paper: elbow for medium lines, shoulder for long ones.
  4. Lower the pen and commit in one pass, at the fastest speed that still lands.
  5. Lift off at the end. Never go back over the line.
Line weight

Weight comes from the nib, not pressure: 05 for silhouettes and the ground line, 03 for secondary edges, 01 for detail and hatching, 005 for distance and texture. A flick (lift off while moving) gives a taper. Exception: if you're unsure of a contour, ink it in 01 first and restate it in 05 at the end.

Exception: with graphite, pressure is a fair way to vary weight. With a fineliner it only crushes the nib, so switch pens instead.

Common mistakes
  • Drawing long lines from the wrist: they curve into arcs
  • Gripping hard or pressing: shaky lines, crushed nib
  • Hesitating mid-stroke: a broken line
  • Re-tracing a missed line: a hairy line that marks the miss
  • Rushing past the endpoint: overshoot
Practice

Parallel lines across a full page of printer paper, no ruler, about 8 mm apart. Then a page at 45° and a page of verticals.

Score: count lines that touch or cross a neighbour. Pass: at most 3 touching or crossing pairs per 25 lines by day 5.

Quick review

RememberThe page records hesitation. Rehearse in the air, commit on the paper.

02 Value through line weeks 4–6

Rule
Tone = fraction of paper covered by ink. You change tone by changing spacing, layers or nib, never pressure.
Variables
Line spacing, hatch angle, layer count, nib size.
Exceptions
Each new cross-hatch layer must turn at least 30° (ideally 45°) from the last; offsets under about 20° make moiré clumps, and stacking at the same angle closes the white gaps unevenly. Mud also comes from uneven spacing and from closing the last white gaps. Solid black is allowed for the deepest shadow.

Lab 03 Hatching lab

Change one variable at a time. The page measures real ink coverage of the swatch, maps it to the 6-step value scale, and computes unevenness (σ of coverage across 784 small cells) for both cross and same-angle layering, so you can see mud as a number.

Phase 2 · Lesson sheet

Understanding Texture

Texture is value plus pattern, so it obeys every Phase 2 rule.

02
Key idea

Every material reaches the eye through two signals: how dark it is (ink coverage) and what its marks look like (pattern). Get the value right first; the pattern only tells the eye which material it is. Analog: value is the measure, pattern is the categorical label.

Texture types
Smooth
Plaster, concrete, painted wood. Long, sparse parallel lines; lots of white.
Rough
Stone, stucco, bark. Stipple and broken outlines; small, sharp shadows.
Patterned
Brick, tile, cladding. Draw the joints, not every unit, and let the pattern fade away from the focal point.
Reflective
Glass, polished metal. The highest contrast in the drawing: dark panes with crisp white streaks, sharp mullions.
Observation notes
  • Light: texture shows in raking light (light skimming across the surface) and flattens in frontal light, however bright.
  • Direction: follow the grain or courses: horizontal for brick and clapboard, vertical for board-and-batten.
  • Scale: units shrink with distance; far away, draw every second or third joint.
  • Contrast: put the darkest darks next to the brightest reflections.
Exception

Many guides teach texture with pencil pressure (light, medium, heavy). That's right for graphite. In ink the same job is done by line spacing and layers, which is what the matrix above shows.

Practice

Draw the matrix on paper: 4 textures × 3 densities in 5 cm squares.

Test: photograph it in grayscale. Each row should read one clear value step darker than the row above. Pass: each row's average gray differs from the next by at least 15% (a photo editor's eyedropper shows it). If two rows look the same, widen the spacing gap between them.

Quick review

RememberSuggest, don't inventory. Three good brick courses say "brick wall" better than three hundred.

Phase 2 · Lesson sheet · used again in Phases 3–4

Combining Textures

One dominant material, one secondary, one accent.

03
Key idea

Materials compete for attention, like series on a chart. Give one material most of the surface, one a supporting role, and keep a small accent. Past four materials, the drawing turns to noise.

How it works
  1. Outline the building in 05 and 03.
  2. Mark material zones lightly in pencil and pick the dominant one.
  3. Texture the dominant material, densest near the focal point, fading outward.
  4. Add the secondary and accent materials over less area.
  5. Finish with tone: eave shadows, recesses, glass.
Rule of thumb

Interior designers split color roughly 60 / 30 / 10: dominant, secondary, accent. It transfers well to materials as a starting split. It's a heuristic, not a law: adjust by eye.

Analog: one primary series, one comparison, one highlight. Never five series at equal weight.

Common mistakes
  • Texturing every surface fully
  • Giving two materials equal visual weight
  • Forgetting that texture changes value: brick reads darker than plaster
Practice

A small house with at least four materials (brick, wood door, glass, tile roof), drawn through the four stages above. Do each stage on a fresh copy so you can compare them side by side.

Test: photograph the finished house and blur it. The dominant material should cover at least half the building, no two materials should look equal, and there should be four or fewer.

Quick review

RememberThe contrast between materials is the point; texturing everything equally erases it.

03 Structure and perspective weeks 7–9

Rule
Parallel edges that recede from you converge to one vanishing point; receding horizontal edges converge on the horizon line at your eye level.
Variables
Horizon height, number of vanishing points, distance between them.
Exceptions
Edges parallel to the picture plane don't recede, so they stay parallel: verticals in 1- and 2-point, and widths in 1-point. Tilt your view up or down and verticals recede too, converging on a third vanishing point (lesson 04). Sloped edges (roofs, stairs) converge to an auxiliary vanishing point above or below the horizon, on the vertical line through the vanishing point of their plan direction. Vanishing points placed too close together distort boxes (test: the near corner of a box's top or bottom face must draw wider than 90°, or the box sits outside your roughly 60° cone of vision; start with vanishing points at least 3× the object's width apart); for buildings they often sit off the page, so tape scrap paper to the sides.

Phase 3 · Lesson sheet

Looking Up & Down

The third vanishing point: what happens when you tilt your view.

04
Key idea

Tilt your view and vertical edges start receding too, so they converge on a third, vertical vanishing point (VVP). Looking up: horizon low, VVP above. Looking down: horizon high, VVP below.

How it works
  1. Place the horizon: low on the page to look up, high to look down.
  2. Put the left and right vanishing points on it, far apart.
  3. Put the VVP on a vertical line, well above or below the page.
  4. Draw the nearest vertical edge first. Aim every vertical at the VVP and every horizontal at its side vanishing point.
  5. Check: extend three verticals. They should meet at one point.
2-point vs. 3-point

In 2-point perspective, verticals stay parallel because your view is level. 3-point is the exception the Phase 3 rule warns about: correct when you tilt your view, wrong when you don't.

When to switch (rule of thumb): once your view tilts more than about 15°, or the subject fills more than about 60° of your vertical view. Exception: architectural drawings often keep verticals parallel for small tilts, like a shift lens, because converging verticals read as leaning.

Analog: the VVP is the same projection applied to the vertical direction vector.

Phone photos of tall buildings are 3-point by accident. That's why the week 9 photos must be shot level.

Common mistakes
  • Putting the VVP too close: towers turn into pyramids
  • Mixing parallel and converging verticals in one drawing
  • Forgetting that horizontals still go to the side vanishing points
Practice

Two drawings as boxes first: one tower looking up from the sidewalk, one block looking down from a rooftop.

Test: extend 3 verticals in each; they should meet within 2% of the page width of one VVP.

Quick review

RememberA level view means parallel verticals; a tilted view means a third vanishing point.

Phase 3 · Lesson sheet · week 9

Building Ornament

Detail on top of structure: envelope, axis, shadow.

05
Key idea

Ornament is the last layer of the pipeline, never the first. Box its envelope, set the symmetry axis, then let shadow shapes, not outlines, carry the carving.

How it works
  1. Envelope: box the whole ornament in pencil.
  2. Axis: draw the center line; most ornament is symmetric.
  3. Big shapes: circles for volutes, arcs for moldings.
  4. Contours in 01, only where an edge turns.
  5. Shadow: hatch the undersides. That's what makes it read as carved.
Vocabulary
Cornice
The projecting crown at the top of a wall.
Dentils
A row of small blocks under a cornice.
Frieze
The band below the cornice, often carved.
Capital
The head of a column or pilaster.
Pilaster
A flat column attached to a wall.
Keystone
The central wedge at the top of an arch.
Pediment hood
A triangular or curved cap over a window or door.
Tips
  • At a distance, reduce ornament to rhythm: dentils become a dashed line, a frieze a texture band.
  • Draw one half, then check the other against the axis.
  • Put full ornament only at the focal point (Selection).
Common mistakes
  • Outlining every leaf
  • Starting with detail before the envelope
  • Ornament everywhere at equal detail
Quick review

Practice: find a cornice, capital or keystone and draw it in the four passes. LA's historic Broadway theater district is full of them; a level phone photo works too. Test: flip your drawing horizontally; the halves should match within 2 mm of the axis, and someone else should tell projecting from recessed parts in at least 4 of 5 elements. Analog: envelope = bounding box, axis = schema, contours = the data.

RememberShadow carves; outline flattens.

Rule

Variables
Focal point location, vignette shape (how the drawing fades out), what to omit.
Exceptions
White paper reads as light, so omitting detail is often the strongest way to show sun. Technical elevations break the rule on purpose.

Concept Sketches

Ideas first, details later: rectangles and triangles only.

06
Key idea

A concept sketch tests one massing idea in under two minutes. It's a prototype, not a deliverable: speed and quantity beat accuracy, which is why it's the one place this program lets lines stay loose.

How it works
  1. Set a timer: 2 minutes per concept.
  2. Start with the elevation: a rectangle plus a triangle or two.
  3. Push it into 2-point perspective as boxes.
  4. Hatch one side so the volume reads.
  5. Move to the next concept; don't fix the last one.
Technical analog

This is rapid prototyping: generate many cheap variants, then develop one, the way you'd sketch several query plans before optimizing the winner.

Common mistakes
  • Detailing too soon
  • Erasing instead of starting a new concept
  • Five versions of the same idea
Practice

Concept sprint: 5 massings in 10 minutes, using only rectangles and triangles. Circle the best one; it becomes your next thumbnail.

Test: blind-rank each sprint the next day. Over 4 sprints the "fifth idea" claim holds if the winner's average position is 3rd or later.

Quick review

RememberQuantity builds quality; the fifth idea usually beats the first.

  3. 2 minutes max per idea. Never fix; start the next one.

5 distinct ideas (different massing, not just different windows), each readable as 3D, in ≤ 10 minutes.

People in Perspective

The horizon rule: eyes on the horizon, size from distance.

07
Key idea

When you stand on the same level ground as the people you draw, every standing adult's eyes sit on the horizon line, near or far. Distance changes their size, never where the horizon crosses them.

How it works
  1. Draw the horizon (your eye level) and the vanishing point.
  2. Place the nearest figure: feet on the ground, eyes on the horizon.
  3. For each new figure, pick the feet point; its eyes still go on the horizon.
  4. Block figures as simple shapes: head, torso, legs.
  5. Overlap a few and vary the spacing; avoid neat rows.
Exceptions

The rule assumes you and the figures share one level ground plane. Change your own height and the horizon still crosses every standing adult at the same body point, just not the eyes: about two-thirds of the way up (belly height) when you sit, above their heads from a second floor. On a slope or stairs the rule breaks: uphill figures' eyes sit above the horizon and downhill figures' below, because they stand on a different plane. Children are shorter, so the horizon passes over their heads.

Common mistakes
  • On flat ground while standing, figures' heads at different heights
  • Over-detailed figures: they're scale markers, not portraits
  • Evenly spaced rows of people
Practice

Add 3–5 people to a street scene with the horizon rule.

Test: lay a ruler along the horizon. It should cross every standing adult at the eyes, within 1 mm. Analog: horizon height is your eye height, a constant joined to every figure.

Quick review

RememberPeople are a scale bar. Get them right and the building's size reads instantly.

Schools of Thought

Every tradition is a different setting of the same five dials.

08
Key idea

A school isn't a mood; it's a configuration. Each tradition turns the same dials (line precision, tonal range, constructed structure, deliberate omission, and speed) to different settings. Studying a school means reading its config, then borrowing one setting at a time.

The dial matrix · tap a school

Dial settings are this guide's judgment from the sources (1 = barely emphasized, 5 = defining), meant for comparison, not measurement.

Find your school

Set the dials to the drawings you admire. The page ranks schools by distance across all five dials (Euclidean, like nearest-neighbour matching).

How to use this
  1. Each Sunday master copy, pick one school and copy one of its drawings.
  2. Before copying, predict its dial settings from the matrix.
  3. After copying, log which dial surprised you.
  4. In the next weekday session, borrow exactly one setting into your own drawing.
  5. Rotate schools; after 10 Sundays you've sampled the whole matrix.

Technique Library

Ways to put tone and form on paper, and when each one wins.

09
Key idea

Every technique is the same function with different arguments: mark shape, spacing, direction and layers in; tone and texture out. Pick a technique by what you need the output to say (flat value, form, material, speed), not by what looks impressive.

Practice

Shade the same simple box three times with three different techniques, aiming for the same three tones (light top, mid left, dark right).

Test: photograph all three in grayscale. If the matching faces read as the same value, you control tone independently of technique. If not, the technique is driving you.

Common mistakes
  • Mixing three or more techniques in one small area
  • Choosing stipple for large areas: beautiful and very slow
  • Hatch direction that ignores the surface it describes

RememberTechnique is a tool for value, never a substitute for it.

Do · Draw this

One box, three techniques

30 min · trains Value · pen + sketchbook

Steps
  1. Draw the same 4 cm box three times.
  2. Shade the dark side of #1 with parallel hatching, #2 with cross-hatch, #3 with stipple.
  3. Aim for the same mid-gray on all three.
  4. Time each one; write the minutes under it.
Pass test

Squint: all three dark sides read as the same value. Note which was fastest for that value: that's your default technique.
